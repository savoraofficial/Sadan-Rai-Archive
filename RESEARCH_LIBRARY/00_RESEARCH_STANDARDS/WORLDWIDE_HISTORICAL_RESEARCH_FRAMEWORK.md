# SADAN RAI ARCHIVE
## Worldwide Historical Research & Evidence Framework

**Purpose:** A practical, transparent research standard for the nine archive collections. This document is a framework and source-discovery register, not a claim that every historical proposition has already been independently verified.

**Editorial principle:** Evidence first; conclusion second. No community, religion, or historical tradition is presumed true or false in advance. A source is used only for claims it can actually support.

6. Tamang History & Civilization

8. Ancient & Medieval History
9. Comparative, World & Belief History

Keep the nine collection identities and their existing content/files separate. Cross-link where evidence genuinely overlaps; do not merge distinct communities or traditions into one undifferentiated narrative.

## 2. Required page structure for every research topic

1. **Research question** — What exactly is being investigated? Define period, place, people, and terms.
2. **Executive summary** — Short, plain-language account for students and general readers.
3. **Context and chronology** — Dates, locations, maps, timeline; mark approximate dates clearly.
4. **Primary evidence** — Inscriptions, original manuscripts, contemporaneous records, archaeological reports, artefacts, dated photographs, original oral-history recordings, and archival documents where available.
5. **Source transcription / translation** — Original wording when legally and practically possible; identify translator, edition, page/folio, uncertainty and editorial interventions.
6. **Scholarly discussion** — Compare specialist books, peer-reviewed articles, university theses, excavation reports and relevant research databases.
7. **Community knowledge and oral history** — Identify community, narrator/knowledge holder (only with consent), location, date, language, interviewer, translator, recording and consent conditions. Do not treat oral tradition as worthless; do not automatically convert it into independently dated political chronology.
8. **Competing interpretations** — Explain what scholars or communities disagree about, their evidence, and the limits of each position.
9. **Claim-by-claim evidence table** — State each proposition separately and link it to the exact evidence.
10. **Research conclusion** — What the reviewed evidence supports, what remains uncertain, and what would change the conclusion.
11. **Limitations and unanswered questions** — Missing documents, inaccessible collections, uncertain dates, translation problems, sampling gaps, and research still needed.
12. **References and further reading** — Full citations, persistent links/DOI/catalogue IDs, page/folio, access date and rights statement.
13. **Revision history** — Version, author/editor, review date, changes, and unresolved corrections.

## 3. Evidence status labels

Use one controlled status for each claim, not a vague overall badge for an entire topic.

- **VERIFIED — SOURCE DETAILS:** bibliographic record or object identity has been checked. This does *not* mean every interpretation of the source is verified.
- **SUPPORTED:** cited evidence directly supports the narrowly worded claim, and the evidence location is recorded.
- **CORROBORATED:** independent sources or evidence types support the claim; explain whether the sources are truly independent.
- **SUPPORTED WITH LIMITATIONS:** evidence supports part of the claim, but its scope, date, interpretation or representativeness is limited.
- **CONTESTED:** credible sources differ; present the strongest evidence and arguments on each side.
- **TRADITION / COMMUNITY ACCOUNT:** a tradition or community account is documented with attribution; independent historical dating may remain unresolved.
- **PRELIMINARY / HYPOTHESIS:** a plausible interpretation that needs more testing.
- **INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE:** the current record cannot responsibly decide the claim.
- **NOT ESTABLISHED:** the reviewed evidence has not established the claim; this is not automatically proof that the claim is false.
- **REJECTED / CORRECTED:** reliable evidence demonstrates a specific error; explain the reason and preserve the correction history.
- **UNREVIEWED:** collected but not assessed.

Never call a source “independent corroboration” if it merely repeats another author. Never treat search-result snippets, social posts, an institution's catalogue title, or the existence of a manuscript as proof of every historical statement made about it.

## 4. Claim register template

For every substantive claim, record:

| Field | Required content |
|---|---|
| Claim ID | Stable unique identifier |
| Exact claim | One testable proposition, not a paragraph containing multiple claims |
| Topic / collection | One of the nine collections; add cross-links if needed |
| Place and time | Geographic scope and date range |
| Source ID | Link to the source register |
| Source type | Primary document, archaeology, oral history, peer-reviewed study, book, catalogue, journalism, social post, etc. |
| Exact locator | Page, folio, inscription number, figure, table, paragraph or timestamp |
| What it supports | Narrow description of the evidence actually present |
| What it does not prove | Important limits or tempting but unsupported inference |
| Independent comparison | Other evidence checked and how independent it is |
| Contradictory evidence | Credible disagreement or counterevidence |
| Rights / consent | Reuse status, licence, consent, privacy restrictions |
| Status | One controlled label above |
| Reviewer / date | Who checked it and when |
| Next action | Specific remaining task |

## 5. Source hierarchy and triangulation

No single universal hierarchy works for every historical question. Choose sources according to the claim.

- **Material and archaeological claims:** excavation reports, context, stratigraphy, dating method, laboratory report, object provenance, conservation record, and independent specialist interpretation.
- **Dated political or administrative claims:** contemporaneous inscriptions, charters, land/revenue records, correspondence, manuscripts and archival documents, read in their historical context.
- **Textual/religious history:** manuscript witnesses, textual variants, dating/provenance scholarship, critical editions, translations, and the distinction between a text's religious meaning and what it can establish as social or political history.
- **Language and identity:** linguistic descriptions, language corpora, historical ethnography, community terminology and self-identification, with dates and regional differences made explicit.
- **Oral traditions:** multiple documented tellings, performance context, narrator/community attribution, language, recording date, transmission history, and careful comparison with external evidence. Oral accounts are evidence of living memory and cultural knowledge; chronological claims need their own evaluation.
- **Modern social history:** interviews, surveys, demographic records and local documents, with sampling and consent limitations described.
- **Social media:** discovery leads, contemporary self-representation and evidence of current discourse. Do not use a viral post as standalone proof of ancient history. Save the original URL, author/account, timestamp, media context and archive copy where lawful; verify images/video provenance and seek independent corroboration.

Triangulate across different evidence types where possible. Three websites copying one unsourced claim are one information lineage, not three independent sources.

## 7. Social media, news, blogs and community websites — how to use them

Include these sources when they contain relevant community knowledge, eyewitness material, contemporary debate, a first-hand interview, a digitised primary document, or a traceable lead. Classify each accurately:

- **Official institutional post:** evidence of what that institution announced, not automatically proof of every historical interpretation in the post.
- **Researcher/community expert post:** useful lead; verify cited publication, source image and credentials/context.
- **Community oral account/video:** preserve attribution, language, date, consent and context; distinguish testimony from independent corroboration.
- **News article:** check named author, editorial process, original interview/document, publication date and corrections.
- **Blog / personal site:** inspect author identity, references, evidence and whether it simply copies another source.
- **Facebook/TikTok/YouTube/X post:** do not use view counts, virality or confident narration as evidence quality. Check original upload, provenance, location/date claims and independent records.
- **AI-generated text/image/audio:** never cite as historical evidence. Trace any factual assertion back to human-authored, identifiable sources.

Respect community protocols and privacy. Do not publish sacred/restricted knowledge, personal data, sensitive archaeological locations or recordings without the relevant permission and consent.

## 8. Citation standard

Use Chicago Notes and Bibliography as the default for historical writing, with consistent bibliography entries. A reference should contain as many of these as applicable:

- Author / institution
- Full title
- Editor, translator or compiler
- Journal/book/collection name
- Publisher and publication year
- Volume, issue and page range
- Exact page, folio, catalogue number, inventory ID, inscription number or timestamp
- DOI or stable institutional URL
- Date accessed for dynamic pages
- Language and translation details
- Copyright / licence / permission status

Never cite a search snippet where the full source can be opened. Do not reproduce long copyrighted passages or restricted manuscript images without permission.

## 9. Research conclusion template

### Findings
State only what the reviewed evidence supports, with citations next to each key finding.

### Strength of evidence
Explain whether the conclusion rests on a contemporary primary record, archaeological context, independent scholarly synthesis, oral tradition, or a combination.

### Alternative interpretations
Summarise credible competing views fairly and identify what evidence each uses.

### Limitations
State missing documents, dating uncertainty, translation issues, access restrictions, source bias, sampling limits and what was not reviewed.

### Conclusion
Use measured language: “The evidence reviewed supports…”, “The record suggests…”, “This remains disputed…”, or “The available sources do not establish…”.

### Further research
List specific next steps, not a generic statement that more research is needed.

**Do not write “100% proven,” “all scholars agree,” or “worldwide research confirms” unless the scope is precisely defined and the evidence truly supports that statement.** A project can aim for 100% inventory coverage and 100% claim-status assignment; it cannot promise that every historical truth is settled.

## 10. Publication quality gate

Before publishing a research page, verify:

- [ ] Every material historical claim has a claim ID and source.
- [ ] Every quotation has an exact locator and is checked against the source.
- [ ] Translations and date conversions are identified.
- [ ] At least one relevant independent source or a clear explanation of why none is available has been recorded.
- [ ] Contradictory evidence and uncertainty are represented fairly.
- [ ] Oral knowledge has attribution, consent and context.
- [ ] Images, scans, maps, audio and video have provenance and rights status.
- [ ] Bibliography links resolve and are not just search-result pages.
- [ ] The conclusion matches the evidence and does not exceed it.
- [ ] Reviewer, review date and revision history are recorded.
- [ ] Public display does not expose private credentials, sensitive personal information or restricted knowledge.

## Current completion statement

This framework and source-discovery map have been prepared. It does **not** claim exhaustive worldwide searching, complete review of every social-media post or article, full reading of every linked source, claim-level verification of every item in the existing archive, fieldwork, peer review, or institutional accreditation. Those require a defined claim inventory and iterative source-by-source review. Maintain the archive's existing research files and update each claim's status only when the corresponding evidence has actually been checked.
